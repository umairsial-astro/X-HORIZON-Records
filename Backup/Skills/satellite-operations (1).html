SKILL / SATELLITE OPERATIONS

Getting a spacecraft to orbit is the easy part. Running it for ten years is the job.

Satellite operations is the discipline of keeping a spacecraft alive, pointed, powered, and useful — from the first signal after separation to the last maneuver before it burns up. This page covers the subsystems, the ground infrastructure, and a working lab bench: real TLE/SGP4 propagation, ground tracks, pass geometry, Doppler, and link budgets.

ANATOMY OF A SATELLITE

Eight subsystems, one spacecraft

Every satellite, from a 1U CubeSat to a multi-tonne GEO comsat, is built from the same functional blocks. The payload does the mission's actual job; everything else exists to keep the payload alive and pointed the right way.

Power (EPS)

Solar arrays, batteries, and regulation. Every other subsystem draws its power budget from here — miss it and nothing else matters.

Propulsion

Thrusters for orbit-raising, station-keeping, and deorbit. Many small satellites fly without any and simply decay.

ADCS

Attitude determination and control: reaction wheels, magnetorquers, star trackers, gyros. Decides which way the satellite is facing.

Thermal

Radiators, heaters, and coatings that keep electronics inside their operating range through sun and eclipse.

C&DH

Command and data handling — the onboard computer. Runs the flight software, timestamps data, executes stored commands.

Communications

Uplink for commands, downlink for telemetry and payload data. Bandwidth here often limits the whole mission's output.

Payload

The reason the satellite exists — an imager, a comms relay, a science instrument. Everything above is overhead in service of this.

Structure

The bus that holds it all together and survives launch loads — vibration, acoustics, and shock — without shaking anything loose.

ORBIT CLASSES

Altitude decides the job

Where you put a satellite determines what it can see, how often it sees it, how long it lasts, and how much it costs to talk to. Operators pick an orbit for the mission, then design everything else around it.

LEO 160 – 2,000 km · 90–120 min period

Earth observation, imaging, and broadband constellations (Starlink, ISS). Close enough for low latency and detail, but any one satellite is only overhead for minutes at a time.

MEO 2,000 – 35,786 km · 2–12 hr period

Navigation constellations — GPS, Galileo, GLONASS. A middle ground: fewer satellites needed for global coverage than LEO, less delay than GEO.

GEO 35,786 km · 23h56m period

Matches Earth's rotation, so the satellite holds one spot in the sky. Used for weather (GOES), broadcast TV, and comms where a fixed ground antenna matters more than low latency.

SSO 600 – 800 km · ~98° inclination

A sun-synchronous LEO tuned so the satellite crosses every latitude at the same local solar time — consistent lighting for imaging satellites, orbit after orbit.

Rings drawn to scale relative to Earth's radius (inner filled circle). GEO sits roughly 5.6 Earth radii out — far enough that one satellite can see a third of the planet at once.

GROUND SEGMENT & TT&C

The half of the mission that never leaves Earth

Telemetry, tracking & command (TT&C) is the operational link between mission control and the spacecraft. Telemetry flows down — subsystem health, sensor readings, event logs. Commands flow up — mode changes, maneuver plans, software patches.

A LEO satellite is usually visible from any single ground station for only a few minutes per pass, several times a day. Operators either build a network of stations spread around the globe, lease time on a shared network, or accept that most of the orbit happens with no one listening — storing data onboard until the next contact.

Between contacts, the spacecraft runs autonomously against a stored command sequence and its own fault-protection logic. If something goes wrong with no ground station in view, the satellite has to detect it, react safely, and wait — see the Mission Operations Simulator below for exactly that scenario.

A contact, step by step:

1. Acquisition of signal (AOS) — the satellite rises above the station's elevation mask, usually 5–10°.
2. Antenna tracks the pass, following azimuth and elevation as the satellite crosses the sky.
3. Stored telemetry and payload data downlink; new commands and ephemeris uplink.
4. Loss of signal (LOS) as the satellite drops below the mask on the other side.

Everything useful has to happen inside that window — which is exactly what the labs below calculate.

MISSION OPERATIONS

What an operator actually does, day to day

ROUTINE

Contact scheduling

Booking ground-station time against every satellite's predicted passes, prioritizing when stations or antennas are shared across a fleet.

ROUTINE

Commanding & telemetry review

Building and validating command sequences before uplink, then confirming they executed correctly from the telemetry that comes back — try it yourself in the Commanding Lab below.

SCHEDULED

Station-keeping

Small, regular burns to counter perturbations — atmospheric drag in LEO, lunar/solar gravity and solar pressure in GEO — that would otherwise drift the satellite out of its assigned slot.

SCHEDULED

Conjunction assessment

Screening the catalog of tracked objects for close approaches and planning a collision-avoidance maneuver when the probability crosses a threshold — modeled in the Conjunction Assessment lab below.

REACTIVE

Anomaly resolution

Diagnosing an unexpected safe-mode entry, power dropout, or attitude fault from limited telemetry, then commanding a recovery — often against the clock of the next pass.

END OF MISSION

End-of-life & deorbit

Passivating batteries and tanks, and either lowering a LEO satellite's orbit to reenter within international guidelines or boosting a GEO satellite to a graveyard orbit.

MISSION LIFECYCLE

Mission timeline

What operators are actually doing at each stage, from launch to disposal.

Operators are largely spectators during ascent — the launch provider owns this phase. The mission team's job is to be ready to receive the first signal within seconds of separation, wherever in the world that first pass happens to occur.
The spacecraft leaves the launch vehicle and, on most missions, boots into a conservative default state — antennas may still be stowed, solar arrays may not be deployed. The very first telemetry confirms the vehicle survived launch loads.
Launch and Early Orbit Phase — the highest-intensity period of the whole mission. Deploy solar arrays and antennas, establish stable attitude control, confirm every subsystem in sequence. Round-the-clock shifts are normal here; a fault this early has the least margin for error.
With the bus stable, the payload is switched on and calibrated — first light for an imager, first lock for a navigation payload. Performance is compared against ground-test predictions before the mission is declared operational.
The routine years: scheduled contacts, payload tasking, telemetry trending, and periodic software updates. Most of a satellite's operational life is spent here, and the goal is for it to be uneventful.
Interleaved with nominal operations for as long as propellant allows: small planned burns that correct the slow drift caused by drag, gravity anomalies, and radiation pressure, keeping the satellite in its assigned slot or ground track.
Not scheduled, but not rare either — every long-lived spacecraft eventually trips a fault protection response. Operators reconstruct what happened from stored telemetry, form a hypothesis, and recover conservatively rather than quickly.
Triggered by propellant depletion, a subsystem failure with no workaround, or simply the planned end of the mission. Operators plan disposal while there's still enough control authority left to execute it safely.
Batteries and tanks are passivated to prevent a future explosion, and the satellite is either lowered to reenter within international 25-year guidelines (LEO) or raised to a graveyard orbit a few hundred kilometers above GEO.

CASE STUDIES

Four missions, four sets of trade-offs

Approximate, educational figures — not live mission data.

CREWED / LEO

ISS

Altitude
~400–420 km
Period
~92.7 min
Inclination
51.6°
Mission purpose
Continuously crewed microgravity research platform and technology testbed.
Operational challenge
Drag at this altitude is strong enough that the station needs periodic reboosts, using either visiting-vehicle thrusters or its own propulsion.
Communications challenge
Relies on a mix of direct-to-ground S/Ku-band and the TDRS relay network for near-continuous coverage rather than waiting for ground-station passes.
Attitude / control
Large, flexible solar arrays and a crewed structure make attitude control a balance between pointing accuracy and structural loads.
Operational lesson
The busiest, most trafficked orbit also has the highest conjunction workload — screening and maneuver planning is a constant background task.
BROADBAND / LEO

Starlink

Altitude
~340–570 km
Constellation
Thousands of satellites
Band
Ku / Ka
Mission purpose
Global broadband internet from a very large low-orbit constellation.
Operational challenge
Operations happen at fleet scale — individual-satellite intervention doesn't scale to thousands of spacecraft, so station-keeping and collision avoidance run as automated policy.
Communications challenge
Phased-array, electronically-steered antennas replace mechanical gimbals so each satellite can serve many ground terminals as it crosses the sky.
Attitude / control
Precise, continuous pointing is required simultaneously for the sun (power), Earth (payload), and orbital velocity vector (drag minimization).
Operational lesson
At megaconstellation scale, the operations problem shifts from "keep this satellite healthy" to "keep the fleet's aggregate behavior safe and predictable."
NAVIGATION / MEO

GPS

Altitude
~20,200 km
Period
~11h58m
Planes
6, evenly spaced
Mission purpose
Global positioning, navigation, and timing via a well-tested constellation geometry.
Operational challenge
Every satellite carries atomic clocks that must be monitored and corrected continuously — clock error translates directly into position error for every user on the ground.
Communications challenge
A sparse, dedicated ground-station network uploads precise orbit and clock corrections rather than handling high-volume data.
Attitude / control
Antenna arrays must stay Earth-pointed through eclipse seasons, when the sun-Earth-satellite geometry makes attitude control noticeably harder.
Operational lesson
The orbit geometry itself — not any single satellite's capability — is what guarantees four-satellite visibility from anywhere on Earth.
WEATHER / GEO

GOES

Altitude
~35,786 km
Period
~23h56m
Coverage
Fixed disk view
Mission purpose
Continuous weather imaging over a fixed third of the planet.
Operational challenge
Station-keeping must hold both longitude and inclination tightly — drift in either degrades the fixed-disk view that the whole mission depends on.
Communications challenge
High-rate imagery downlink runs continuously to a dedicated ground segment rather than in short discrete passes, since the satellite never sets.
Attitude / control
Extremely fine, stable pointing is required so imagery lines up correctly frame to frame — any residual jitter shows up directly as a blurred image.
Operational lesson
GEO trades orbital variety for view continuity — the entire mission concept only works because the satellite holds still relative to the ground.
EQUATION REFERENCE

The six equations that come up constantly

Orbital velocity (circular)

v = √(μ/r)
v — speed, km/s
μ — Earth's gravitational parameter, 398,600.4 km³/s²
r — orbit radius from Earth's center, km
Worked example
r = 6,371 + 500 = 6,871 km → v = √(398600.4 / 6871) ≈ 7.61 km/s

Orbital period

T = 2π√(a³/μ)
T — period, seconds
a — semi-major axis, km (= r for circular orbits)
μ — gravitational parameter, km³/s²
Worked example
a = 6,871 km → T ≈ 5,676 s ≈ 94.6 min

Free-space path loss

FSPL = 20log₁₀(d) + 20log₁₀(f) + 32.44
FSPL — loss, dB
d — distance, km
f — frequency, MHz
Worked example
d = 1,700 km, f = 435 MHz → FSPL ≈ 149.7 dB

Thermal noise power

N = kTB
N — noise power, W
k — Boltzmann's constant, 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K
T — system noise temperature, K
B — bandwidth, Hz
Worked example
T = 300 K, B = 9.6 kHz → N ≈ 4.0×10⁻¹⁷ W ≈ −134 dBm

Doppler shift

Δf = f₀(vr/c)
Δf — frequency shift, Hz
f₀ — transmitted frequency, Hz
vr — radial (line-of-sight) velocity, m/s — negative while closing
c — speed of light, 3×10⁸ m/s
Worked example
f₀ = 435 MHz, vr = −7 km/s → Δf ≈ +10.2 kHz (received frequency higher)

Link margin

Margin = (C/N)available − (C/N)required
Margin — dB above (or below) the threshold needed for the link to close
(C/N)required — set by the modulation and coding scheme's target bit-error rate
Worked example
Available 22 dB, required 10 dB → margin = +12 dB, comfortably positive
GLOSSARY

Terms that come up constantly

AOS / LOS
Acquisition of signal / loss of signal — the start and end of a ground-station contact.
TLE
Two-line element set — a compact, standard text format encoding a satellite's orbit at a given epoch.
OMM
Orbit Mean-Elements Message — a structured (XML/JSON/KVN) successor to the TLE format carrying the same kind of mean orbital elements.
SGP4
The standard propagator used with TLEs, including drag and major perturbations — more accurate than two-body over time.
TT&C
Telemetry, tracking & command — the operational radio link between mission control and the spacecraft.
Apogee / perigee
The highest and lowest points of an orbit around Earth.
Inclination
The tilt of an orbital plane relative to Earth's equator, in degrees.
RAAN
Right ascension of the ascending node — where an orbit crosses the equator going north, measured in inertial space.
Ground track
The path traced on Earth's surface directly beneath a satellite as it orbits.
Nadir / zenith
Straight down toward Earth's center, and straight up away from it.
Slant range
The straight-line distance from a ground station to a satellite — always longer than the altitude except directly overhead.
Doppler shift
The frequency shift caused by relative motion between satellite and ground station — significant enough in LEO that receivers must track it.
Link budget
The full accounting of gains and losses in a radio link, from transmitter power to receiver noise floor.
EIRP
Effective isotropic radiated power — transmit power combined with antenna gain and feed losses, as if radiated equally in all directions.
FSPL
Free-space path loss — signal attenuation from distance alone, before atmosphere or hardware losses.
C/N
Carrier-to-noise ratio — how far the received signal sits above the noise floor.
Eb/N0
Energy per bit over noise density — the metric that actually predicts bit-error rate for a given modulation.
Station-keeping
Small periodic maneuvers that hold a satellite in its assigned orbital slot against perturbations.
Conjunction assessment
Screening for close approaches with other tracked objects and deciding whether to maneuver.
TCA
Time of closest approach — the moment two tracked objects reach minimum separation in a conjunction event.
Delta-v
The change in velocity a maneuver requires — the fundamental "fuel currency" of spaceflight.
Bus
The spacecraft platform — structure, power, propulsion, ADCS, C&DH — that carries the payload.
Uplink / downlink
Radio traffic going up to the satellite (commands) and down to Earth (telemetry, data).
Sun-synchronous orbit
A LEO inclination tuned so the satellite crosses each latitude at a consistent local solar time.
Geostationary
A circular, equatorial GEO orbit where the satellite appears fixed over one point on Earth.
ADCS
Attitude determination and control system — sensors and actuators that point the spacecraft.
EPS
Electrical power system — solar arrays, batteries, and power regulation.
Safe mode
A minimal, power- and pointing-conservative fallback state the spacecraft enters automatically when it detects a serious fault.
Anomaly
Any unplanned deviation from expected spacecraft behavior — the trigger for an ops investigation.
LEOP
Launch and Early Orbit Phase — the intensive first days of a mission spent deploying and checking out the spacecraft.
Command sequence
An ordered list of commands, often validated against spacecraft state constraints before being uplinked.
