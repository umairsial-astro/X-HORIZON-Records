The grammar of evidence.

Scientific writing has one job: let a stranger verify your claim without asking you a single question. This guide covers the structure, sentence-level habits, and citation mechanics that make research papers, lab reports, and technical notes hold up under scrutiny.

Structure

Anatomy of a paper

Most experimental papers follow IMRaD. Reviewers read in this order and judge whether each section does its one job — not the paper's job as a whole.

01

Title & Abstract

Contains: question, method, headline result, ~250 words
The abstract is read ten times more often than the paper. It should stand alone: state the problem, what you did, what you found, in that order.
02

Introduction

Contains: background, gap, hypothesis
Narrows from field to gap to question. Ends with the specific hypothesis or objective — not a restatement of the whole field's history.
03

Methods

Contains: materials, procedure, analysis plan
Written so a competent stranger could reproduce the work without contacting you. Past tense, passive voice is acceptable here where the actor is irrelevant.
04

Results

Contains: findings only, no interpretation
Report what happened, with figures carrying the detail. Interpretation belongs in Discussion — mixing the two is the most common structural fault.
05

Discussion

Contains: interpretation, limitations, context
Explains what the results mean, how they compare to prior work, and where they could be wrong. A paper with no limitations paragraph reads as naive.
06

Conclusion & References

Contains: takeaway, full citation list
One paragraph answering the original question directly, followed by a reference list formatted to the target journal's exact style — see Citation below.
Workflow

The writing process

Good scientific writing is rewritten, not written. Treat drafting and editing as separate, non-overlapping stages.

Draft

Get the argument down. Write Methods and Results first — they're the least ambiguous.

Revise

Check the argument, not the sentences. Does each section support the one claim in the abstract?

Edit

Sentence-level pass: cut hedges, fix tense consistency, enforce one idea per sentence.

Review

A colleague reads for gaps you can't see. Address every comment, even ones you disagree with.

Sentence level

Style & precision

Clarity is a technical requirement, not a stylistic preference. Five habits do most of the work.

    Prefer precision to hedging

    Say what you actually found. Reserve hedges (may, could, suggests) for genuine uncertainty, not politeness.

    avoidThe results seemed to possibly indicate a trend.
    preferTemperature increased by 2.1 °C over the sampling period (p < 0.01).
    Use active voice where accountability matters

    Passive voice hides the actor. Fine for Methods; a liability in Discussion, where the reader needs to know who is claiming what.

    avoidIt is believed that the effect is caused by X.
    preferWe attribute the effect to X.
    One idea per sentence

    A sentence carrying two claims forces the reader to untangle which evidence supports which. Split it.

    avoidThe sample degraded over time, which we attribute to humidity, and this affected the later trials.
    preferThe sample degraded over time. We attribute this to ambient humidity, which affected later trials.
    Fix terminology once, then keep it

    Elegant-variation — swapping synonyms to avoid repetition — reads as imprecise in science. Name a thing once, then reuse that exact term.

    avoidthe sample … the specimen … the test material
    preferthe sample … the sample … the sample
    Quantify instead of qualifying

    "Significant" and "large" mean nothing without a number attached. Give the magnitude and its uncertainty.

    avoidPerformance improved significantly after calibration.
    preferPerformance improved by 34% (±3%) after calibration.

    Reference

    Glossary

    IMRaD
    Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion — the standard structure for empirical papers.
    Preprint
    A manuscript shared publicly before peer review, usually on a server like arXiv.
    Peer review
    Independent expert evaluation of a manuscript before a journal accepts it.
    DOI
    Digital Object Identifier — a permanent link to a specific published work.
    Corresponding author
    The author responsible for communication with the journal and readers after publication.
    Null hypothesis
    The default assumption of no effect, which a study's evidence attempts to reject.
    p-value
    The probability of seeing a result this extreme if the null hypothesis were true.
    Reproducibility
    The ability of an independent researcher to obtain the same result using the same methods.
