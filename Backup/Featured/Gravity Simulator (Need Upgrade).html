About this model

Every body attracts every other body by F = G·m₁·m₂/r², summed pairwise each step — a direct N-body integration, the same approach used in early solar-system numerical studies before tree/mesh approximations were needed for large N.

Forces use a small Plummer softening term (r² → r² + ε²) so two bodies passing very close don't produce an unbounded, timestep-breaking force spike. It's a standard technique in N-body codes, and it only matters during close encounters.

Two unit systems are used depending on scenario: Astronomical (AU, solar masses, years, with G = 4π² — the convention that makes Kepler's third law read P² = a³ exactly for a one-solar-mass primary), and Normalized (G = 1, dimensionless), used for the classical figure-eight and Pythagorean three-body configurations as originally published.

Three integrators are offered. Velocity Verlet is symplectic — it conserves energy on average over long integrations, which is why it's the default for orbital mechanics. RK4 is more accurate step-to-step but slowly leaks energy over many orbits. Euler is included mainly so you can watch it visibly drift — a good demonstration of why naive integration fails for orbits.

Multi-planet scenarios place orbits coplanar with circular velocities, a simplifying approximation — real orbits are mildly eccentric and inclined by a few degrees.

Timestep is fixed by default (each scenario ships with a step size verified numerically for stable, energy-conserving integration). Adaptive mode instead bounds the step by the shortest local two-body crossing/free-fall timescale each step, shrinking automatically during close or fast encounters — clamped between 1/40× and 6× the scenario's base step so it can never collapse to zero or run away.

Collisions default to Ignore (bodies can pass through each other, as in the idealized point-mass problems this lab is built around). Merge conserves mass and linear momentum exactly and removes the absorbed body. Elastic resolves an idealized 1D elastic collision along the line of centers, conserving both momentum and kinetic energy exactly. Both use a stylized collision radius proportional to mass^(1/3) — not each body's real physical radius, since real planetary radii are far smaller than the orbital distances shown here.

Orbital elements shown for a selected body are osculating two-body elements — computed instant-by-instant against the most massive other body present, as if every other perturber vanished right now. In a true N-body system (more than two significant masses) these drift continuously and are not exact closed-form orbits; they're labeled "osculating" for that reason.

Surface imagery for the Sun, Earth, Moon and the eight planets is real: photographic texture maps built from NASA elevation and imagery data (Solar System Scope, CC BY 4.0), loaded live where possible and bundled directly into this file for the Sun, Earth and Moon so they always work offline. If a texture can't load, that body falls back automatically to a procedurally generated approximation — never a blank or broken sphere. The Black Hole scenario is a stylized, physically-informed visualization (event horizon, photon ring, Doppler-tinted accretion disk), not a general-relativistic ray trace — this engine is Newtonian throughout and does not model light-bending, frame-dragging, or time dilation. Its Schwarzschild radius/ISCO reference figures (in the Selected Body panel) are real formulas evaluated as if its mass were that many solar masses; the scene's own coordinate distances are normalized and not to scale. See "Field reference" and each body's "Real-world reference" panel for exact sourcing.
